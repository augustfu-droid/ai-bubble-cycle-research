# Author's View: Technology Will Change the World, but Each Investment Still Has to Pay Back

Original research baseline: October 31, 2026 (Beijing time) | Original timing forecast registered: November 1 | Event update: November 17, 2026

**I see the first half of 2027 through the first half of 2028 as the main risk window for the current AI capital bubble to burst on a broader scale.** Here, a "burst" means sustained valuation repricing across multiple entities, corroborated by pressures on investment, financing and recovery of capital from assets. A single stock-price decline does not satisfy that definition, nor would a bubble burst mean AI technology has lost its value.

This is the author's subjective forecast, registered afresh on November 1 using evidence available through October 31, 2026. It is not a date precisely calculated by a data model, and carries no uncalibrated probability or forecast decline.

| Scenario and possible timing | Triggers and transmission I anticipate | What would change my judgment |
|---|---|---|
| **Baseline: H1 2027 to H1 2028** | Collections continue to lag; depreciation on assets entering service and firm payment commitments increase, with downward revisions to expected returns affecting valuations and investment | Revenue and cash catch up with investment, while older assets continue earning sustainable returns |
| Earlier credit shock: November 2026 to Q1 2027 | A major customer's payments or refinancing are disrupted, with pressure spreading through procurement, collateral and guarantees | Long-term funding is secured and payments resume, without the shock spreading across entities |
| Delayed amplification: H2 2028 to H1 2029 | Financing continues to support expansion while new firm obligations still outpace collections, ultimately creating greater capital-recovery pressure | Expansion slows and shortfalls narrow, so the extended cycle allows investment to be absorbed rather than pressure to accumulate |
| Absorption, avoiding a full-scale bubble burst: observe 2027-2029 | Payments and efficiency gains absorb earlier investment, construction slows in an orderly way and cash improves | Net cash persistently lags, while renewals and financing deteriorate again |

These are four possible paths, not probabilities that can be added together. **If the baseline's repricing across multiple entities and transmission through funding and operations have not appeared by June 30, 2028, I will record that window as unfulfilled. I cannot automatically extend it and count the forecast as correct.**

**Since April, I have consistently argued that "delay amplifies risk."** This remains my central risk judgment, but it is conditional: cash receipts from commercialization fail to keep pace with continuing investment, while companies keep adding equipment, lease and financing commitments. Current evidence supports divergence among companies and localized accumulation of capital-recovery pressure; it does not yet show an industry-wide simultaneous collapse of demand and financing.

## How I Assess Different Situations

I do not place technological progress and capital recovery on the same axis. The four cells are assessed for the same business, investment cohort and observation period. "Recovery keeps pace" means ongoing net cash collected from customers can cover the planned recovery of investment and firm payments falling due that have not already been counted. "Recovery lags" means this operating cash is insufficient and additional funding is still needed. The pace of technological progress is assessed through acceptance of newly enabled tasks, reliability and full delivery costs; slower progress does not mean existing capabilities disappear. Cash from legacy businesses and financing provide a buffer, but do not establish payback for new AI assets or justify moving into the left-hand column.

| Technological progress / recovery through customer payments | Recovery keeps pace, supported by customer cash | Recovery lags, requiring additional funding |
|---|---|---|
| **Continuing technological breakthroughs** | New tasks expand what can be billed, potentially allowing existing investment to be absorbed. Stronger delivery and distribution provide a buffer; those unable to keep pace with falling costs may still come under pressure. If renewals and gross profit on a cash basis weaken after subsidies are withdrawn, I will revise this assessment downward. | Application providers and customers may benefit first, while older equipment, high-cost models and heavily invested projects come under pressure. If lower prices expand usage and net cash from older equipment can also cover investment and payments as planned, I will shift toward an absorption assessment. |
| **Slower technological progress** | Those with established paying use cases and continuing cash generation have more room to wait; highly valued projects dependent on the next capability leap come under pressure first. If existing-customer renewals or cash from the core business weaken, the buffer assessment no longer holds. | Those with concentrated customers, heavy near-term payments and high refinancing dependence are more likely to experience funding pressure first; those with ample cash and the ability to stop expansion have a relative buffer. If payment terms improve and secured funding covers the shortfall, I will lower my assessment of liquidity risk; moving into the left-hand column still requires operating recovery to keep pace. |

The four cells describe economic states, while the four paths on the opening page describe timing. Entities can move between cells; a cell cannot be mapped to a single year, added into probabilities or used to predetermine company winners and losers. **The cell that warrants the most caution is the one in which technology advances markedly, yet shareholders who bought older assets may receive no incremental cash.**

## Nine Core Judgments I Retain

**1. I acknowledge real operating profit, but do not let investment revaluation stand as evidence of operating performance.** Cloud services, chips, advertising and applications already have verifiable revenue, profit and cash. "All the profit is fake" cannot explain those facts. But net income that includes equity revaluation cannot directly establish sustained profitability in the core business, much less payback on the next round of construction. Revaluation gains and losses must be adjusted symmetrically, without mixing pretax and after-tax amounts in the subtraction. Removing a single gain also does not produce a fully normalized profit figure. I first ask what customers bought, then examine operating profit, collections and construction payments. (Chapter 1)

**2. I believe the most important question about capital circulation is who ultimately pays.** Cloud providers investing in model companies, model companies buying compute, and equipment vendors providing investment or credit support can enable real delivery; these arrangements do not by themselves make the revenue fraudulent. But upstream revenue recognition does not mean customers outside that circle have provided sufficient, sustainable cash. Equity investment, loans, procurement and guarantees cannot be added into a single total of money "already received"; successful financing is not operating payback either. If customers can later continue buying from operating budgets, the support serves as a bridge. If each round of purchases needs the next round of capital contributions, the risk remains within the chain. (Chapter 2)

**3. I am more concerned about a mismatch between the pace of recovery and firm payments; GPU depreciation will carry the pressure into profit.** Microsoft still had a cash surplus after construction spending in its latest fiscal quarter; Alphabet had a quarterly shortfall but remained positive over the trailing year; Amazon's construction spending exceeded operating cash over the trailing year. These cannot be combined into a conclusion that "the industry's cash has run dry." Construction is paid for first and depreciation enters profit later. If rental prices, utilization and economic lives fall short of expectations, equipment may fail to pay back even while it remains operational. Depreciation is not another payment: free cash flow that already deducts cash equipment spending cannot deduct the full cost again. A single quarter of negative cash is insufficient to establish failure; persistent dependence on new money to pay existing commitments is more dangerous. (Chapters 1 and 13)

**4. I do not simply regard AGI or efficiency breakthroughs as a cure for the bubble.** A leap in capability can create new tasks, demand and revenue. It can also reduce the expensive models and hardware needed for the same result, depressing rental prices for older equipment and model fees. Application customers may get cheaper tools while owners of older capital suffer. Conversely, routing, caching and better utilization can extend the period over which older equipment earns revenue. I therefore no longer use a single AGI year to determine valuations: I examine full costs for tasks of equal quality, billable volume and who retains the profit. A technological breakthrough truly helps this investment cohort pay back only when new demand and cost improvements ultimately leave cash in the business. (Chapters 7 and 8)

**5. I insist on differentiation among companies and no longer write the same ending for the "Magnificent Seven."** Platforms with cash from advertising, software and established cloud businesses generally have more room to wait than construction projects dependent on a single customer and refinancing. But platforms also incur procurement, lease and equity-financing costs. Independent model providers must bear R&D, inference and competitive price cuts; asset-heavy cloud providers must align lease commencement, debt and equipment lives; application providers compete for customer budgets, distribution and delivery profit. These are conditional differences in resilience, not a fixed risk ranking. Suppliers earning money today are not immune to customers cutting orders. Holding cash can create consolidation opportunities, but acquisition prices and inherited contractual burdens must still be deducted. (Chapters 10 and 12)

**6. I am raising my assessment of application potential, especially microdramas and coding, but do not equate a larger market with benefits for every vendor.** Finished microdramas, advertising assets and localization can generate repeat purchases. Seedance's external fees and ByteDance's internal efficiency, MiniMax's service revenue and R&D burden, and Kling's revenue and standalone profit should each be examined separately. Coding will first affect hiring, departures that are not replaced and standardized outsourcing, then gradually affect existing headcount. Hours saved yield cash benefits only when they become lower payments or new cash receipts. Knowledge, education and other applications with existing payment relationships deserve research priority, while licensing, review, customer acquisition and bundled suites will still take a share of the benefits. (Chapters 3-6)

There is one explicit correction here: multiplying the number of professional developers by a fixed low monthly fee, then treating the result as a hard ceiling for the entire coding market, was wrong. **V1.5 already withdrew that ceiling.** Usage-based billing, long tasks and nonprofessional uses expand the scope of what can be billed. That does not establish that actual revenue after removing double counting exceeds the old calculation, much less that any tool's valuation is reasonable.

**7. I believe international competition must be examined layer by layer, and opportunities in China must also show up in company accounts.** Similar model capabilities do not imply identical training hardware, engineering ecosystems, energy or capital conditions; restrictions on some chips do not mean China's AI demand disappears. Domestic substitution can generate orders, but compatibility, migration costs, delivery capacity and customer budgets determine how much profit remains. Overseas construction activity must pass through specific products, supplier shares, selling prices and collections before it reaches Chinese companies' earnings per share. Tighter policy may encourage substitution but also damage upstream supply; easing may benefit users while putting local suppliers under pressure. I no longer assume every policy scenario produces investment returns. (Chapters 8 and 9)

**8. I acknowledge that power, sovereign capital and policy can extend the cycle, but do not treat them as unconditional backstops.** Power bottlenecks may protect capacity already connected to the grid, or keep capital tied up longer in projects under construction. Sovereign or long-term funding may help worthwhile projects survive the construction period, or continue supporting low-return expansion. Actual capital contributions, drawdowns, commissioning and risk sharing must be examined; planned amounts cannot be treated as cash. Energy breakthroughs warrant reassessment of costs, but cannot pay today's rent and maturing debt in advance. If competition passes lower electricity prices on to customers, shareholders may retain none of the benefit. (Chapters 8 and 13 and Appendix F)

**9. I separate technological success, operating success and investment success.** Railways, the internet and shale oil teach me that society can gain cheaper basic services without the original investors necessarily making money. They cannot supply a fixed decline, amplification multiple or bottom-buying date for this cycle. For investors without a position, I look for companies that can create benefits for customers and retain cash themselves, at a purchase price that makes sense. For existing holders, I examine cash per share, debt and dilution. Even a good company can be bought too expensively, and issuing new shares does not necessarily destroy value. The key is whether the returns purchased with new money cover the additional claims and costs. (Chapter 10 and Appendices C and D)

What would change my judgment? If customers renew after subsidies are withdrawn, full delivery costs fall, older equipment continues contributing cash, and operating cash still grows as construction slows, I should acknowledge that time is helping absorb the investment. Simultaneous weaker collections, lower renewal prices and restricted financing across several comparable entities, with transmission between them, would be closer to the industry-wide contraction in the forecast. **I am raising my assessment of application potential while continuing to test capital recovery, and I accept responsibility if the timing forecast is not fulfilled.**

## November 17 Update: Governance Changes Call for Earlier Tests of Financing Continuity

**Jensen Huang's departure adds a potential trigger for an earlier adjustment in the AI capital cycle, but I am not changing the main window on the opening page for now, or counting a stock-price decline as a successful forecast.** I am giving higher monitoring priority to the "earlier credit shock from November 2026 to Q1 2027" path, while retaining the countercase of a stable succession, continued third-party financing and improved cash from deliberately slowing expansion. I am not reallocating uncalibrated probabilities here.

The SEC filing confirms that Huang resigned as president, CEO and a director on November 11, with the announcement on November 12; Kress serves as interim CEO while remaining CFO. The company says its product and customer commitments continue, but actual delivery remains to be verified. Existing CUDA assets, systems and teams do not disappear that day, although new investments, guarantees and financing arrangements may be reviewed. I am more concerned about changes in coordination across institutions and the assumption of risk than about treating one person's reputation as the entirety of the company's technological assets. [Leadership 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000082/nvda-20261111.htm)

Chapter 14 separates NVIDIA's construction for its own use, customer support and contingent guarantees, and adds CoreWeave's new Q3 results and a purely hypothetical cash-flow example. Strong operating cash at a supplier and customers' need to finance expansion can both be true. Available credit is not cash, and a prepayment contract becoming cancelable does not mean a refund has already occurred. Securing financing can improve liquidity, but still does not establish operating payback.

The contemporaneous BIS remote-compute rule has been announced and is scheduled for publication on November 19; it is not yet effective as of this research date. It should be analyzed through the specific equipment, customer eligibility, end uses and contractual terms, rather than described as a ban on all generative APIs, microdrama applications or coding applications. [Federal Register public inspection notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/public-inspection/2026-23529/addressing-remote-access-to-advanced-semiconductors)

My additional judgment is this: **governance changes at a key financing node may bring capital-recovery pressure to an earlier test; whether this develops into an industry contraction depends on whether new funding, contract performance and net customer collections continue to align.** Continued financing supports the delayed-amplification case only if net firm obligations grow faster still. Reducing uneconomic expansion and adjusting payments could instead lower risk. The next checks will use formal disclosures, project performance and subsequent quarters, without prematurely using NVIDIA results due for release only on November 18 Beijing time. The nine core judgments, both matrices and my responsibility to record the window as unfulfilled on June 30, 2028 if it does not materialize all remain intact.

This update covers governance, related financial and financing matters, and regulation. Microdramas, coding and other applications retain their original research cutoff; I am not claiming that all of them were reverified on November 17.
## November 17 Competition Update: Another Chip Does Not Mean Another Payer

**I am raising my assessment of the practical substitution capabilities of AMD, TPUs and cloud providers' internally developed chips, while drawing a clearer distinction between NVIDIA-specific risk and industry demand risk.** Real production cases show that a second source can take on some tasks and reduce procurement premiums. But if the same model companies and cloud customers cut their budgets, multiple platforms can come under pressure together. The previous edition already covered TPU system sales, AMD warrants and Broadcom notes. This update adds their effects on the wider system; it does not attribute partnerships predating November 11 to Huang's departure. [Meta production-training research](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.22230v1)

I believe competition will change who captures profit, without predetermining that every challenger benefits. AMD can win orders while price cuts, engineering support and customer incentives put pressure on profit. TPUs can improve Google's internal costs, cloud services or system sales, but standalone product profit has not been disclosed. Migration must also account for networking, software, reliability and acceptance costs; vendor specifications cannot simply be turned into a ranking of winners. [Alphabet Q3 discussion](https://abc.xyz/investor/events/event-details/2026/2026-Q3-Earnings-Call-2026-Ujou16CTw9/)

More fundamentally, AMD already has compute commitments and guarantees, and some TPUs sold externally by Alphabet still carry repurchase support. Diversifying technology and delivery can reduce dependence on a single point, yet may not diversify the credit risk of common payers. If lower costs expand paid usage and preserve net cash from older equipment, they help the industry absorb prior investment. If they first depress returns on older GPUs while new construction and financing obligations continue to outpace collections, they may instead amplify risk after a delay. Chapter 14 adds product-status checks, distinctions among funding arrangements and tests of counterevidence. The nine judgments, both matrices, the main window and my accountability on June 30, 2028 remain unchanged.
