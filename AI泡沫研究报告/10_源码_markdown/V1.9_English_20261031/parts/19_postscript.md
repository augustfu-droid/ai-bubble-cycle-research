## Research Closing: Let Subsequent Facts Test the Judgment

Whether AI can transform production and everyday life, whether AI infrastructure can earn back its cost of capital, and whether a security is worth owning at a particular price are three related questions that cannot substitute for one another. Substantive technological progress will not make every investment pay off; excessive capital-market pricing will not erase technological capabilities that have already been developed. Separating these three questions is the only way to preserve room for discussion between optimism about the industry and financial caution.

The most important work in this research is not to find a sufficiently eye-catching score, but to trace a unit of capital from financing, procurement, construction and delivery through customer use, revenue recognition, cash collection and debt repayment. Each stage in the chain has its own dates and evidence: an announced contract is not money received; pledged equipment is not cash that can be realized at any time; revenue growth is not free-cash-flow growth; and a falling share price does not mean the business has already failed. Only by checking each item can we determine whether risk remains a matter of valuation or is entering the company's ability to meet its payments.

Research of this kind will not produce an answer that never changes. If paid demand expands, customer collections improve, and unit-cost reductions are not entirely passed on through price competition, existing capacity may be absorbed over time, and the previously cautious judgment must weaken accordingly. If long-term payment commitments continue to grow while actual use, profits and collections fail to keep pace, risk may merely have moved elsewhere, even if financing remains readily available. Contradictory facts are not the enemy of a conclusion; they are a necessary condition for keeping a judgment valid.

The role of historical analogies also ends there. Shale oil, the internet and other capital cycles can draw attention to financing maturities, the inertia of capacity expansion and the distribution of technological gains, but they cannot assign bankruptcy dates to today's companies, still less guarantee returns to today's investors. Today's business models, cash reserves, policy environment and market structure all require fresh examination. Similarities supply the questions; differences determine the answers.

This report therefore retains its unresolved questions: some contractual terms remain undisclosed; gaps remain between usable project capacity and actual billable utilization; there is no uniform formula for allocating social productivity gains and industry-chain profits; and financial institutions' ultimate look-through risk exposure is still not fully visible. These gaps should neither be concealed by a precise probability nor filled with promotional certainty. They should become the starting point for the next round of evidence gathering.

What readers should ultimately take away is a method of judgment that can be reviewed: knowing what they believe, which document, calculation and assumption that belief depends on, and what evidence should make them change their minds. The market will not prove a view on a research report's timetable. Research can, however, hold itself to a stricter standard of responsibility: do not present the unknown as known, a scenario as a prophecy, or analysis as action beyond the authority granted.
