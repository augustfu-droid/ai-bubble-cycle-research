## Chapter 12: Risk-Transmission Scenarios—From Operating Deviations to Asset Repricing

A fall in one company's share price does not establish that the AI capital cycle has begun clearing; record supplier revenue does not establish that downstream projects have paid back. This chapter unfolds the process missing between those statements: which change occurs first, whose profit, assets and cash are affected, how long financing can cushion the problem, and under what conditions it spreads. What follows is conditional stress testing, not a crash date, an index target or a fitted probability forecast.

### 12.1 Identify the Starting Point Before Combining Different Shocks into One Crisis

Disappointing demand, greater technological efficiency, more expensive funding and undeliverable projects can all reduce some asset prices, but have different implications for the industry. Customers no longer willing to pay imply damage to revenue; customers willing to pay but unable to obtain electricity and equipment imply delayed recovery; cheaper execution of the same task may hurt old-asset rents while giving application providers a larger market. Calling all of these "a collapse in AI demand" misses who actually bears the losses.

| Conditional scenario | Initial deviation | Company variable to check first | Evidence that may interrupt transmission |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue catches up slowly | Paid retention, expansion or customer-project returns fall short of budget | Contract renewals, net revenue retention, receivables and customer cash gross profit | Billable usage expands while collections and unit profit improve together |
| Efficiency-driven repricing | New technology lowers costs for equivalent-quality tasks, pressuring old-product pricing | Older-equipment rents, product mix and replacement capital expenditure | New demand absorbs capacity and repurposed older equipment still generates positive cash |
| Financing and macroeconomic reinforcement | Interest rates, credit spreads or lending terms deteriorate | Future payments, maturity structure, restricted cash and collateral headroom | Long-term financing is secured and operating improvements reduce dependence on new borrowing |
| Delivery or rule shock | Grid connections, acceptance, export licenses or customer migration are obstructed | Construction in progress, prepayments, default liabilities and revenue start dates | Construction resumes, customers accept delivery, and alternative supply is economical |
| Investment is gradually absorbed | Demand and cash recovery outpace new investment | Comparable cash gross profit, asset turnover, maintenance investment and return on capital | This disconfirms the pessimistic hypothesis; it cannot be relabeled risk that is "merely delayed" |

These scenarios may overlap, but overlap does not mean probabilities can be added. For example, efficiency gains may first depress older GPU rents, then weaken collateral values and finally tighten borrowing. That is one continuous causal chain; a loss model cannot count each of its three stages as a full separate shock.

### 12.2 Path One: Customer Budgets Weaken First, Without Immediate Deterioration in Financial Statements

This scenario begins when end customers reduce payments for the same quality and service scope, or demand lower prices at renewal. Free users, trial requests and total tokens may still grow, so paid usage, contractual discounts, refunds, plan migrations and renewals must be considered together. Neither declining usage without price data nor listed price cuts without customer-mix information is enough to establish lower recoverable revenue.

The earliest income-statement change may appear in gross profit rather than revenue: previously contracted revenue is still recognized, while service costs rise because of reasoning depth, support staff or promotional subsidies. Application providers then reduce discretionary compute purchases; cloud providers first face changes in utilization and unit pricing; equipment suppliers may see the effects later in orders, lead times or inventory. Minimum-purchase contracts and customer prepayments can separate these changes in time; one cannot assume that every company along the chain weakens in the same quarter.

The balance sheet usually supplies the other half of the picture: whether receivables grow faster than revenue, contract assets turn into receivables, prepayments are being consumed, and customers need new external financing to settle old bills. The cash flow statement tests whether these changes are merely payment-term arrangements or have created an operating funding gap. Recognizing revenue before collecting cash is not itself abnormal; persistent divergence without subsequent collections warrants lowering recovery assumptions.

NVIDIA's FY27Q2 ended July 26, 2026 is an observation requiring this layered reconciliation: revenue rose 18% sequentially, operating cash fell from USD 50.344B in the preceding quarter to USD 24.077B, and days sales outstanding rose from 45 to 60. The company also explained the effects of large-customer payment terms, working capital and cash taxes. This supports continued examination of subsequent collections, not classifying new receivables directly as bad debts, nor excluding cash pressure solely because sales grew. [NVIDIA, FY27Q2 CFO commentary](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000073/q2fy27cfocommentary.htm)

The point at which this transmission stops is straightforward: customers pay as agreed, renewals expand, service costs fall, and incremental revenue no longer depends on greater subsidies. If such evidence appears, demand or cash judgments should be revised; an initial expectation of decline does not justify preclassifying every new dollar of revenue as circular financing.

### 12.3 Path Two: Demand Keeps Growing While Older Assets Earn Less

Cheaper models and hardware may allow usage to keep growing while reducing unit returns on existing assets. The central question is whether usage growth and cost improvements offset lower prices, not whether technological progress is "good" or "bad." For application customers, a cheaper task may increase adoption. For operators that bought expensive equipment and locked in long-term liabilities, the same change may shorten the competitive-use window and make investment harder to recover.

Assume a service originally charges 1 per unit, has variable cost of 0.6, and supplies 100 units, yielding contribution margin of 40. If the price falls to 0.8, cost falls to 0.45 and usage rises to 130, contribution margin becomes 45.5. If cost falls only to 0.55, contribution margin is 32.5. Revenue rises from 100 to 104 in both cases, but the unit-economics conclusions are opposite. This illustration excludes fixed expenses, tax and reinvestment; it is not any company's actual gross profit.

The income statement may first show deeper discounts and lower gross profit, followed later by changed depreciation estimates or asset impairment. Balance-sheet carrying values may not change immediately even as secondhand equipment, re-leasing prices and collateral valuations reprice. Cash still has to cover maintenance, energy, rent and scheduled principal repayments. Stopping some workloads saves variable costs but does not automatically release all contracts.

Economic life, accounting depreciation life and financing maturity must be separated. A new product launch does not scrap old equipment that day. Redeployment into batching, less latency-sensitive tasks or another customer requires validation of software compatibility, energy efficiency, memory and retrofit costs. If cash coverage after repurposing remains adequate, the assumption that "old assets rapidly go to zero" should weaken. Conversely, if renewal revenue can no longer cover maintenance and debt, declining economic value should be recognized even before accounting impairment.

### 12.4 Path Three: Refinancing Stops Cushioning the Problem, Turning Operating Stress into Payment Stress

A funding shock usually first hurts those required to pay on specified dates without sufficient collections yet in place. This is an analytical condition, not proof that every emerging compute company is riskier than a large cloud provider. Research must compare deployable cash, facilities with draw conditions already satisfied, customer prepayments and future mandatory payments, rather than rank companies solely by debt balances.

CoreWeave disclosed on August 10, 2026 that the DDTL 5.5 loan has a term of approximately five years, while the underlying customer contracts average approximately three years, indicating that some debt service depends on renewals, re-leasing or other funding sources. This does not mean revenue must vanish in years four and five, but requires renewal prices, asset residual values, customer concentration and refinancing conditions to enter the stress table together. [CoreWeave, USD 2.6 billion loan announcement, 2026-08-10](https://investors.coreweave.com/news/news-details/2026/CoreWeave-Closes-2-6-Billion-Loan-Facility-Expanding-Financing-Flexibility-for-AI-Infrastructure/default.aspx)

Profit and cash may move in different directions under financing pressure. Extending debt maturity can reduce near-term principal payments, and equity issuance can bring cash, but interest, dilution and added restrictions change per-share value. If financing merely pays irrevocable expenditure for continued expansion, the net buffer may not improve. A large contract likewise does not guarantee drawable credit: a loan may require asset delivery, collateral coverage or customer-credit conditions.

Macroeconomic inputs cannot be read in only one direction. On October 28, the Federal Reserve voted 10 to 2 to maintain the target range at 3.75%–4.00%; the two dissenters favored a 25-basis-point increase. This is a published hold decision, no longer a pending event, and cannot be interpreted as a turn to rate cuts. The US Treasury's October 30 two-year nominal, ten-year nominal and ten-year real yields were 4.56%, 4.94% and 2.60%, down 12, 6 and 6 basis points respectively from October 26. The policy rate, Treasury yields, corporate credit spreads and outstanding fixed coupons are four distinct variables: falling Treasury yields ease the discount benchmark but do not establish improved credit access, collateral haircuts or refinancing conditions for vulnerable projects. [Federal Reserve, 2026-10-28 decision](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20261028a.htm); [US Treasury, October 2026 nominal yields](https://home.treasury.gov/resource-center/data-chart-center/interest-rates/TextView?field_tdr_date_value_month=202610&type=daily_treasury_yield_curve) and [real yields](https://home.treasury.gov/resource-center/data-chart-center/interest-rates/TextView?field_tdr_date_value_month=202610&type=daily_treasury_real_yield_curve)

CoreWeave still completed a USD 4.2 billion convertible-bond issuance on September 22, also showing that the financing window remained usable. But funds raised and capital recovered are different questions; successful issuance cannot replace validation of equipment and customer profitability. [CoreWeave, 2026-09-22 issuance-completion filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1769628/000176962826000432/crwv-20260917.htm)

Confirming credit contagion requires evidence that different customers and financiers are affected in common—for example, simultaneous deterioration in borrowing terms, collateral haircuts, loan renewals or payments at several companies. A widening credit spread at one company, especially with sparse quotes or changed debt maturities, cannot automatically become a uniform default probability. Without a complete holder and guarantee chain, one cannot assert that risk stops at technology companies or that it must become a banking crisis.

### 12.5 How Risk Passes Through the Three Financial Statements

| Shock | Income statement | Balance sheet | Cash flow and funding sources | Common verification error |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Customer order cuts or price reductions | Lower revenue, gross profit or future guidance | Changes in receivables, inventories and contract assets | Lower collections and working capital tied up | Current-quarter stocking and payment-term changes do not equal bad debts |
| Delayed lease commencement or acceptance | Delayed revenue recognition and changed expense-recognition timing | More construction in progress and prepayments | Payments made before recovery begins | Construction payments do not mean assets are operating |
| Falling older-equipment rents | Lower gross profit, possibly adjusted depreciation or impairment | Pressure on asset and collateral values | Lower re-leasing receipts and demands for additional collateral | Impairment is not a same-day cash outflow, but may affect financing |
| Lower investee valuations | Revaluation gains/losses or other changes depending on accounting classification | Investment carrying values adjusted | Without a sale or contribution, no equal cash outflow is necessary | Pretax items cannot be divided directly by after-tax net income |
| Debt or equity financing | Interest and per-share metrics may change | Cash, debt or equity increases | Receipts temporarily cushion the shortfall | Filing, signing, settlement and cash receipt are different milestones |
| Backstops or guarantees triggered | Expenses or provisions may be recognized | Contingent obligations become liabilities | Cash expenditure occurs when payment conditions are met | The nominal ceiling is not a current loss |

This table is a modeling interface, not one risk label for every company. Meta's company-defined 2026Q3 free cash flow was USD −2.120B, already deducting cash asset purchases/construction and finance-lease principal; cumulative FCF for the first three quarters remained USD +11.049B. One negative quarter deserves monitoring, but subtracting CapEx from FCF again would exaggerate the gap, and legal expenses and prior-year tax items cannot all be explained as deterioration in AI operations. [Meta, 2026-10-21 Q3 results and non-GAAP reconciliation](https://investor.atmeta.com/investor-news/press-release-details/2026/Meta-Reports-Third-Quarter-2026-Results/default.aspx)

Anthropic's public S-1 dated October 26, 2026 supplies a constraint in the other direction: first-half recognized revenue of USD 16.286B and operating cash flow of USD 3.929B demonstrate actual revenue and operating collections. The same document's large net loss includes noncash financing revaluation and cannot be called an equal amount of cash burn. Long-term purchases, cancellation rights and equipment leases still require period-by-period matching; both sides must enter the model. [Anthropic, S-1, MD&A cash flows and contractual commitments on pages 171–173](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/anthropicpbc-sx1.htm)

### 12.6 From Companies to Stocks to Indices: Make the Arithmetic Reconcile

Earnings changes are not share-price changes. For profitable companies using consistent measures, `share-price change factor＝EPS change factor×P/E change factor` provides a first approximation. EPS must also reflect issuance, buybacks and non-operating items; P/E cannot be applied mechanically to loss-making companies.

A pure illustration: EPS falls 20% and P/E falls 25%, giving a price factor of `0.80×0.75＝0.60`, a 40% decline, not the 45% obtained by adding the two. This expression only shows price sensitivity to given assumptions; it does not establish that those assumptions will occur. If a higher discount rate has already been used to reduce the multiple, another full "interest-rate shock decline" is not added separately.

Approximate index return can be calculated using beginning weights as `Σ w_i×r_i`, stating the observation period and whether rebalancing, dividends and corporate actions are ignored. For example, assume a group represents 30% of the index and falls 40%, while the other constituents fall 10%. The index return is `30%×(−40%)+70%×(−10%)＝−19%`. The 30% is only an illustrative input, not a verified current weight in any index. Deriving a larger decline requires explicit changes to other constituents' earnings or valuation assumptions, not adding the same wealth effect again to a price path that already contains it.

When a stock falls in a capitalization-weighted index, its weight changes with its price; this does not itself require a fully replicating fund to sell more of it. Additional selling pressure requires behavioral evidence such as net redemptions, cash settlement, derivatives hedging, leverage constraints or investor risk adjustments. Secondary-market turnover of ETF units also does not mean an equal sale of constituents by the fund. Concentration implies shared risk exposure, but index ownership alone cannot establish that "passive investing must produce a stampede."

Private assets have a different timing pattern. Financing-round valuations are not continuous transaction prices, and preferences, liquidation terms and secondary-share discounts also affect comparisons. A down round may change a listed shareholder's carrying value, but the amount, taxes and recognition timing depend on the applicable accounting treatment; a valuation reduction does not necessarily erase the same amount of profit. Conversely, a successful listing can improve price discovery and financing without automatically proving that the issue valuation is reasonable.

### 12.7 When an Industry Adjustment Becomes Macroeconomic Reinforcement

Corporate capital-expenditure cuts affect equipment, construction, energy and employment; lower stock-market wealth may also change consumption. But market-capitalization losses are not contemporaneous GDP losses, corporate revenues include intermediate inputs, and overseas-produced equipment also involves an import deduction. Without domestic value-added measures, affected sectors and fund flows, a large global CapEx number cannot be divided directly by US GDP to estimate the depth of a recession.

Further spread requires new connections: creditor losses erode capital, fund redemptions force sales of unrelated assets, falling collateral triggers additional security, or banks reduce other lending as credit worsens. These are paths to investigate, not facts this chapter has confirmed as occurring. Advertising, software and other cloud revenue at mature platforms can cushion AI-project pressure; nonbank credit, project finance and guarantee chains may also carry losses beyond the platforms. Evidence from only one side cannot determine the ceiling of systemic risk.

Sovereign investors likewise cannot uniformly be treated as capital that will never exit. Equity, fiscal procurement, loans, guarantees and policy intentions materialize differently. Strategic goals may lengthen evaluation horizons without removing budget, technology-license, construction and customer-demand constraints. Track payments made, draw conditions and project milestones; a national-project name does not replace a guarantee document.

If an adjustment occurs, its consequences need not be limited to an industry-wide contraction. Operators with weaker financing may sell assets; companies with stronger cash and customer resources may consolidate capacity; lower compute prices may increase application adoption. The same change can benefit consumers while imposing losses on older-asset financiers. This does not allow winners to be named in advance, because acquisition prices, retrofit costs, contractual burdens and competition still determine cash returns.

Regional divergence also requires item-by-item argument. Customer budgets, technology access, energy costs and policy support across economies may cause adjustments to occur at different times. These differences neither guarantee a natural advantage for one region nor insulate its stock market from global funding changes. Index constituents may change with competition, but that is not the same conclusion as "the logic of index investing has now failed," which requires independent evidence on diversification, costs and long-term returns.

### 12.8 Leading, Coincident and Lagging: Update on Evidence, Not a Scenario's Timetable

| Observation layer | Changes that may appear earlier | Subsequent validation | Lag that should not be assumed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customer operations | Trial-to-paid conversion, renewal discounts and customer-budget changes | Revenue recognition, subsequent collections and cash gross profit | Every customer cuts orders in the same quarter |
| Asset delivery | Construction milestones, power connections and equipment acceptance | Lease commencement, billable utilization and operating/maintenance costs | Announced operations mean immediate full load |
| Financing terms | New loan quotes, collateral requirements and covenant adjustments | Actual draws, maturity renewals and payments | CDS leads stock prices by a fixed number of months |
| Financial reporting | Changes in payment terms, capitalization and estimates | Impairment, profit/loss recognition and cash payments | The accounting recognition date is the start of economic loss |
| Market pricing | Changes in expectations and risk premiums | Whether orders, cash and financing follow | An earlier share-price fall proves the fundamental forecast correct |

For each event, record at least the original expectation, actual result, parameter changed and disconfirming evidence; do not automatically declare a systemic crisis on "any two signals." A prediction that fails to occur within its original window is scored under its original conditions. Later new risks need a new argument; the clock cannot be reset.

As of October 31, 2026, the latest cloud-provider results make "divergence between demand growth and cash recovery" a more direct observation: Microsoft still has a positive cash surplus, Amazon's trailing-twelve-month free-cash-flow shortfall has widened, and Alphabet also requires operating performance to be separated from investment revaluation and construction expenditure. Specific figures appear in the profit chapter. These findings raise the priority of testing funding coverage, but do not independently establish a collapse in industry-wide end demand or a general financing interruption. The next check is whether sustainable operations and secured funding can cover the gaps, and whether pressure expands into common orders, credit and payments across multiple companies. Without that evidence chain, localized pressure is not described as the arrival of a systemic crisis.
